"GI-tagged" appears on a lot of Indian food packaging now, usually in gold, usually near a
seal-shaped graphic. It is one of the few claims on an Indian spice pack that has actual
legal machinery behind it — and also one of the most widely misread. A GI tag is not a
quality grade. It is not an organic certificate. It does not mean a laboratory tested the
contents of the packet in your hand.

It means something narrower and, once you understand it, more useful.

## What a GI actually is

A Geographical Indication is a form of intellectual property. It reserves a product name for
producers in one defined geographical area, where a given quality, reputation or
characteristic of that product is essentially attributable to that place.

In India, GIs are granted under the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and
Protection) Act, 1999, and administered by the Geographical Indications Registry in Chennai
under the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks. Darjeeling tea was the
first registered Indian GI. Several hundred have followed, covering food, textiles,
handicrafts and agricultural goods.

The mechanism is closer to Champagne than to a food-safety mark. Sparkling wine made in
Sussex cannot be called Champagne, however good it is, because the name belongs to a place.
In exactly the same way, turmeric grown in Karnataka cannot legally be sold as Lakadong
turmeric, however similar the plant.

## What it guarantees — and what it doesn't

This is the part worth being precise about, because the gap between the two is where most
misleading marketing lives.

**A GI tag guarantees:**

- The product genuinely comes from the registered geographical area.
- The registered production method — documented in the GI application — was followed.
- The name is legally protected, so a producer outside the area using it can be acted against.
- A collective of producers in that region, not a single company, holds the right to use it.

**A GI tag does not guarantee:**

- Any particular grade, potency or purity of the packet you bought. A GI covers the name, not
  the batch.
- That the product is organic. Those are entirely separate certifications with separate audits.
- That nothing was added after the crop left the region. Adulteration during processing and
  packing is a different problem, policed by food-safety law, not by GI law.
- That the price is fair, or that the farmer was paid well.

That last point deserves emphasis. A GI is a name protection, not a fair-trade scheme. It
creates the *possibility* of better returns for a region by making its name valuable and
harder to copy, but it does not by itself put money in a grower's hands. What determines that
is who buys, at what price, and through how many intermediaries.

## Why it matters anyway

Given all those exclusions, is a GI worth caring about? Yes — for one reason that outweighs
the rest.

**A GI makes origin a checkable fact rather than a marketing adjective.**

Almost every other claim on a spice pack is soft. "Premium" means nothing. "Farm fresh"
means nothing. "Authentic" means nothing. "Natural" means nearly nothing. Origin, once it is
tied to a registered GI, is different: either the crop came from the defined district or the
name is being used unlawfully. It converts a vibe into a claim someone can be held to.

That matters most for foods where place genuinely changes the product. Lakadong turmeric
carries seven to twelve per cent curcumin because of altitude, soil and a growing season
three months longer than the plains — [move the cultivar and the number
collapses](/blog/lakadong-vs-kandhamal-turmeric). [Byadagi
chilli](/products/byadagi-chilli-powder) is grown for pigment rather than heat, and the
pigment concentration is specific to its belt in Haveri district. [Mithila
makhana](/products/mithila-makhana) depends on a pond-farming and hand-popping tradition
that exists in north Bihar and effectively nowhere else. In each case the place is not
decoration; it is the explanation.

## How to read a GI claim without being fooled

Four practical habits.

**Look for the specific registered name, not the vibe.** "Odisha turmeric" is a
geography. "Kandhamal Haladi" is a registered indication. The first is a description anyone
can print; the second is a legal claim. Vagueness where precision was available is usually
deliberate.

**Check whether other claims are being smuggled in.** A pack can be honestly GI-tagged and
still be adulterated, over-milled or a year old. The GI covers where it came from. It says
nothing about what happened afterwards.

**Be suspicious of a GI name on something suspiciously cheap.** GI crops are usually
harder to grow, in smaller volumes, on more difficult terrain. That is generally why the
name became worth protecting. A Lakadong price that matches commodity turmeric is telling
you something.

**Ask who they buy from.** A GI is held collectively by producers in the region. A seller
who cannot say which collective, cooperative or farmer producer organisation they buy through
is probably buying from a trader who bought from a trader — which may still be genuine, but
nobody in that chain can tell you so.

## The honest limit

A GI tag is a floor, not a ceiling. It tells you the geography is real and the traditional
method is on record. Everything above that floor — how fresh the grind is, whether the crop
was dried in the sun or a machine, whether anything was blended in afterwards, what the
farmer was actually paid — is a matter of who you buy from and what they are willing to tell
you.

That is why we name the district on every pack, publish the curcumin range rather than a
single flattering figure, and say plainly when a claim is about tradition rather than
certification. The tag is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.
