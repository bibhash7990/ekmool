Ask which turmeric is "better" and you will get an argument. Ask which one belongs in
your kitchen and the question becomes answerable, because Lakadong and Kandhamal are not
really competing for the same job. One is a high-curcumin specialty from the wettest hills
in India; the other is an everyday cooking turmeric grown without chemical inputs on tribal
farms in Odisha. Both carry a Geographical Indication. Both are genuinely single-origin.
They behave completely differently in a pan.

Here is what actually separates them.

## The short answer

If you are buying turmeric to cook with every day — dal, sabzi, marinades, anything where
turmeric is one of six spices — buy [Kandhamal](/products/kandhamal-turmeric-powder). It is
rounder, earthier, less assertive, and about half the price, so you can be generous with it.

If you are buying turmeric for its curcumin — golden milk, turmeric tonics, or simply
because you want the most concentrated version of the thing — buy
[Lakadong](/products/lakadong-turmeric-powder). You will use noticeably less of it, which
narrows the price gap more than the label suggests.

A lot of households end up keeping both, and that is a perfectly sensible outcome rather
than a marketing line. They are used for different things.

## Curcumin: the number everyone quotes

Curcumin is the compound responsible for turmeric's colour and most of its research
interest, and it is the single clearest difference between these two.

| | Kandhamal | Lakadong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curcumin | above 3% | 7–12% |
| Origin | Kandhamal district, Odisha | Lakadong villages, Jaintia Hills, Meghalaya |
| Altitude | 300–1,100 m | 1,000–1,400 m |
| Growing season | ~8 months | 9–10 months |
| Heat / bitterness | mild, earthy | sharp, resinous, bitter finish |
| Colour | saffron-orange | deep burnt orange |

For context, ordinary commodity turmeric — the kind sold loose by weight, often blended
across several states — typically tests between one and three per cent. So Kandhamal sits
at the top of the normal range, and Lakadong sits several times above it.

The reason for Lakadong's number is not a treatment or an additive. It is a landrace
cultivar that has adapted to one specific set of conditions: high altitude, acidic soil rich
in organic matter, and extremely heavy rainfall. It also takes nine to ten months to mature,
considerably longer than plains turmeric, and that extended season is when curcumin
accumulates. Move the same seed to lower altitude and you get a plant that looks identical
and tests far weaker. This is exactly the kind of place-dependence that a Geographical
Indication exists to protect — [we wrote separately about what a GI tag does and does not
guarantee](/blog/what-is-a-gi-tag).

## What they taste like

Curcumin percentages are easy to print and easy to over-weight. In practice, flavour is what
you will notice first.

**Kandhamal** is warm and earthy with a faint pepperiness, and it sits underneath other
spices rather than on top of them. That is precisely what you want in daily cooking, where
turmeric is a base note supporting cumin, coriander, chilli and asafoetida rather than
leading the dish. It is forgiving: a heavy hand will not ruin anything.

**Lakadong** is sharper and more resinous, with a distinct bitterness at the back of the
palate. That bitterness is the curcumin, and it is the honest signal that you have bought
what you think you bought. In a subtle dish it can dominate. In golden milk, where turmeric
is the entire point, it is exactly right.

The colours differ too, and visibly. Kandhamal is a saffron-orange. Lakadong is deeper —
closer to paprika than to the pale yellow most Indian kitchens are used to. Put a spoon of
each side by side on a white plate and you will not need to be told which is which.

## How much to use

This is where the price comparison gets more interesting than a per-kilo figure suggests.

Because Lakadong runs three to five times the curcumin of ordinary turmeric and carries a
correspondingly stronger flavour, **use about half of what a recipe specifies**. A dish
calling for one teaspoon of turmeric is usually well served by half a teaspoon of Lakadong.
Start lower than you think and adjust upward — the bitterness is easy to overshoot and
impossible to take back out.

Kandhamal you can use one for one with whatever turmeric you were buying before. A rounded
half-teaspoon handles a pot of dal for four.

Run those numbers against pack prices and Lakadong stops looking like a luxury and starts
looking like a concentrate. It still costs more per serving, but not by the multiple the
shelf price implies.

## Why the price gap exists

Lakadong is more expensive for reasons that are structural rather than positional.

The plots are small and terraced on steep hill slopes, which rules out machinery. The
growing season runs three months longer than plains turmeric, so the same land yields less
per year. Harvest is manual. Drying is done in the sun on bamboo racks rather than in hot-air
dryers — slower, weather-dependent, and the reason the volatile oils survive at all. Yields
per acre are lower and labour per kilo is higher at every step.

Kandhamal's economics are gentler, but its farming is no less deliberate. The Kondh
community grows it on plots that rarely exceed an acre, with no synthetic fertiliser or
pesticide — not because a certificate requires it but because the practice never included
them. The soil is fed with cattle manure and forest leaf litter, and the crop is rotated
with pulses and millets. That makes Kandhamal organic by inheritance rather than by
conversion, which is a meaningfully different thing from a farm that switched three years
ago to qualify for a label.

## How to tell if what you bought is real

Three checks, none of which require a laboratory.

**Colour.** Real Lakadong is deep burnt orange. If it looks like the bright chrome yellow of
supermarket haldi, it is either not Lakadong or it has been cut. Kandhamal should be a warm
saffron-orange, not pale.

**Smell.** Sun-dried turmeric smells strongly of earth and warm pepper the moment the pack
opens. Machine-dried turmeric has had most of its volatile oils driven off and smells of
very little. This is the single most reliable test.

**Consistency across orders.** Counter-intuitively, powder that looks *exactly* the same
every single time is a warning sign, not a reassurance. Natural turmeric colour shifts with
harvest lot, rainfall and drying conditions. Perfect batch-to-batch uniformity usually means
the colour has been standardised. Ours vary a little across a season, and we would rather
explain that than fix it.

## So which should you buy?

Buy **Kandhamal** if you cook Indian food regularly and want a turmeric you can use freely
without thinking about it. It is the better everyday jar, and the organic-by-tradition
sourcing is a real thing rather than a claim bolted on at packing.

Buy **Lakadong** if curcumin is the reason you are shopping — for golden milk, for tonics,
or because you want the most concentrated turmeric grown in India and are happy to use less
of it.

Buy both if you want to settle it yourself, which is genuinely the most useful experiment
here. Make the same dal twice, one with each, a week apart. The difference is not subtle,
and after that you will not need anyone's comparison table.
