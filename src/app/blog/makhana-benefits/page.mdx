Most people meet makhana as a bowl of pale, weightless puffs at a wedding or a fasting meal,
and assume it is a kind of popcorn. It is not. It comes out of standing water, is harvested
by divers working blind in chest-deep mud, and is popped one seed at a time by hand because
nobody has managed to build a machine that does it properly.

Understanding that changes how you think about the price — and about what you are eating.

## What makhana actually is

Makhana is the popped seed of *Euryale ferox*, a spiny aquatic plant related to the water
lily. It grows in shallow ponds, and roughly nine-tenths of the world's supply comes from
the Mithila region of north Bihar, where it has been farmed for centuries. In 2022 the crop
received a Geographical Indication under the name **Mithila Makhana**, formalising what the
region's markets had recognised for far longer.

You will also see it sold as fox nut, phool makhana, lotus seed or gorgon nut. Lotus seed is
strictly a misnomer — different plant, different family habit — but it has stuck.

## Why it is the most labour-intensive snack in India

Almost every step resists mechanisation, which is the whole story of its price.

**Harvest.** The seeds sink and settle into the pond bed beneath a canopy of thorned leaves.
Divers from the Mallah community work by feel, in chest-deep water, bringing up seeds by hand
across repeated passes over several weeks. There is no machine that does this. There is
barely a tool.

**Grading and drying.** Seeds are washed, sorted by size, then sun-dried and tempered — a
staged process, because moisture content at the moment of popping determines whether the
kernel bursts cleanly or scorches.

**Roasting and popping.** The seeds are roasted in shallow iron pans, then each one is struck
with a wooden mallet at exactly the right instant so the hard black shell fractures and the
white kernel bursts free. One seed, one strike. Too early and the kernel stays trapped; too
late and it burns. A skilled popper works through thousands in a day and the timing window is
measured in seconds.

That is what you are paying for. Not packaging, not branding — hands.

## What "phool" grade means

*Phool* means flower, and phool makhana is the large, fully-burst grade: the pops that
opened cleanly and evenly. Size correlates with quality here for a real reason — a large pop
indicates the seed was mature and struck at the right moment, and it yields a lighter, more
even crunch. Bihar's own mandis price by grade, and [we buy large grade
only](/products/mithila-makhana).

Smaller, denser, partially-opened pops are cheaper and perfectly edible, but they are chewy
rather than crisp and they absorb oil differently when roasted.

## The nutrition, stated carefully

Makhana is naturally low in fat and a source of plant protein, and it is largely
carbohydrate. Ours arrives entirely plain — no oil, no salt, no seasoning, nothing added
beyond what the popping itself required — so whatever ends up on it is your decision.

Indian households have traditionally used it as a light evening snack, in kheer, and during
fasting periods when grains are avoided. It is filling out of proportion to its weight, which
is most of its appeal: a large bowl is a small amount of food.

We will stop there deliberately. You will find a great many pages claiming makhana treats or
prevents specific conditions. We sell food, not medicine, and specific health outcomes are a
conversation for you and a dietitian or doctor. What we can tell you with confidence is where
it was grown, how it was processed, and that nothing was added to it.

## Six ways to cook it

Plain makhana is a starting point rather than a finished snack. It is bland on its own, which
is exactly why it takes flavour so well.

**1 · Ghee-roasted, the standard.** Heat a teaspoon of ghee in a dry pan, add the makhana,
and toss over low heat for five minutes until they squeak against the pan. Salt while hot.
That squeak is the signal — it means the last of the moisture has gone.

**2 · Masala makhana.** As above, then toss with chaat masala, a pinch of amchur and a little
[Byadagi chilli powder](/products/byadagi-chilli-powder). Byadagi rather than a hotter
chilli, so you get colour and warmth without the burn overwhelming something this delicate.

**3 · Makhana kheer.** Simmer in full-fat milk with cardamom until they swell and soften,
about fifteen minutes, then sweeten. They thicken the milk on their own — no cornflour, no
condensed milk.

**4 · In a curry, like paneer.** Roast first, then add to a tomato-onion gravy in the last
few minutes so they absorb sauce without collapsing. Makhana matar is the classic pairing.

**5 · Turmeric and black pepper.** Ghee-roast, then toss with a small pinch of [Lakadong
turmeric](/products/lakadong-turmeric-powder) and coarse black pepper. Use less turmeric than
you think — Lakadong is strong and its bitterness will take over.

**6 · Caramel, for a treat.** Melt jaggery with a spoon of ghee until it threads, fold in
roasted makhana, and spread to cool on a greased plate. It sets into something between a
brittle and a puffed-rice bar.

## Buying and storing

**Buy large grade.** The size difference is visible in the pack and audible when you eat it.

**Buy plain.** Pre-seasoned makhana is usually seasoned to disguise older or lower-grade
stock, and the oil it carries shortens its life considerably.

**Store airtight, at room temperature.** Humidity is the only real enemy. A glass jar with a
good seal keeps them crisp for several months.

**Revive rather than discard.** If they go soft during a humid stretch, three or four minutes
in a dry pan over low heat brings the crunch straight back. This works every time and almost
nobody knows it.

## Why we sell it

Turmeric and chilli are the obvious things for a single-origin Indian food brand to sell.
Makhana is here because it is the clearest example we know of a food whose price is almost
entirely labour, performed by a specific community, in a specific place, using a technique
that has resisted a century of attempts to automate it.

A GI tag on that is not a marketing flourish. It is the difference between a Mithila diver's
work being recognised in the price and being invisible in it.
